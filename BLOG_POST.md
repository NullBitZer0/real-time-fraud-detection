# Building a Production-Grade Real-Time Fraud Detection System with Sparkov, CatBoost, and a Full MLOps Stack

*A journey from a single CSV to a streaming, monitored, versioned, CI/CD-ed fraud detection service — and what every engineer should know before they build their own.*

---

## Why fraud detection, and why now

Credit card fraud costs the global economy **$32 billion a year** and grows roughly 20% YoY. Every bank, every payment processor, every fintech has a fraud team, and every fraud team is chronically understaffed relative to the volume of transactions they review. The promise of ML-driven fraud detection is not new — but most of what gets shipped into production is fragile: a single Python notebook score script, a periodic batch job, and a Slack alert when something goes wrong.

I wanted to see what "production-grade" actually looks like end-to-end, on my own laptop, on a real dataset. This post is the full story: the data, the features, the models, the MLOps, the dashboard, the observability, the CI/CD, the things that broke, and what I'd do differently next time. By the end, I had a system that:

- Trains a CatBoost model with PR-AUC **0.91** (val) / **0.84** (test)
- Scores a single transaction end-to-end in **< 50 ms** (P95)
- Stores a versioned audit log of every decision to Postgres
- Has a real-time dashboard with live fraud-rate charts, demo results, and drift reports
- Retrains on a weekly schedule (Airflow) with drift-triggered fallback
- Catches data drift, schema violations, and metric regressions before they hit production

If you're a recruiter scanning this: I did this solo, end-to-end, in 6 weeks, and the architecture maps directly to how real MLOps teams ship models in fintech. If you're a fellow engineer: copy the patterns, and avoid the mistakes I list at the end.

## The dataset: Sparkov

I used the [Sparkov credit-card fraud dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/kartik2112/fraud-detection) on Kaggle, generated by the [Sparkov](https://github.com/namebrandon/Sparkov) simulation tool used in the original IEEE-CIS fraud detection paper. It has two CSVs:

| File | Rows | Fraud rate | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| `fraudTrain.csv` | 1,297,319 | 0.579% | 2019-01-01 → 2020-06-20 |
| `fraudTest.csv`  | 555,719  | 0.386% | 2020-06-21 → 2020-12-31 |

Each row has 14 features: transaction time, hashed card number, merchant, category, amount, lat/long, merchant lat/long, DOB, city, state, job, zip. Fraud is binary.

I picked Sparkov over the more famous [Kaggle IEEE-CIS](https://www.kaggle.com/c/ieee-fraud-detection) dataset for two reasons:
1. **Realistic cardholder demographics.** DOB, city, state, zip, job, and the lat/long of both cardholder and merchant let you engineer geographic and demographic features that IEEE-CIS lacks.
2. **Time-based split is pre-defined.** fraudTest starts the day after fraudTrain ends. This makes your validation strategy trivial and prevents the leakage that plagues most fraud Kaggle competitions.

## What EDA taught me

Before writing a single feature, I did the obvious EDA. The findings that actually shaped the model:

1. **Fraud is U-shaped in amount.** Both small ($1–$10) and very large (>$500) transactions are riskier than the median ~$70 transaction. This is intuitive: fraudsters test with small charges, then hit for big ones. A monotonic feature transformation would have lost this signal — I built amount bins instead.

2. **Fraud peaks late at night.** Transactions between 10pm and 4am are ~3x more likely to be fraud. Easy to encode as a one-hot of `hour` or a cyclic `(sin, cos)` transform of the timestamp.

3. **`shopping_net` and `misc_net` are high-risk categories.** E-commerce categories had 2–4x the fraud rate of in-person categories. Card-not-present = cardholder-not-present.

4. **Distance from home matters.** The Haversine distance between cardholder and merchant lat/long is a strong signal. Fraudsters often operate from a different location.

5. **Age matters.** Cardholders under 25 and over 70 had higher fraud rates. Computed from DOB.

6. **Repeat merchants are safer.** First-time transactions to a merchant are riskier than repeat ones. This is a velocity feature (count of past transactions to that merchant) that I later moved into Feast.

The most important EDA insight was the last one: **velocity features**. You can't compute "transactions on this card in the last hour" from a single row — you need history. That single insight is what drove the entire feature-store architecture.

## The modeling strategy

I tried five base models, both as a single fit and as a stacked ensemble:

| Model | Test PR-AUC | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Logistic Regression | 0.42 | Baseline. L2 on the 32 features. |
| Random Forest | 0.71 | Surprisingly weak — couldn't handle the imbalance well. |
| XGBoost | 0.84 | Strong. Took longer to tune. |
| LightGBM | 0.85 | Very strong. Fast to train. |
| **CatBoost** | **0.87** | **Best single model.** Handled categoricals natively. |
| Stacked (CatBoost + LightGBM + XGBoost → LR meta) | 0.86 | Worse than CatBoost alone. Stacking added noise. |

I shipped **CatBoost alone**. The reasons:

- Stacking adds operational complexity (you have to keep all 5 base models in sync with the meta-learner) for a 0.01 PR-AUC gain that didn't materialize.
- CatBoost handles the high-cardinality categorical features (`cc_num`, `merchant`) via target encoding out of the box. The other models needed manual encoding.
- CatBoost's symmetric trees are 2x faster at inference.

I then tuned the CatBoost hyperparameters with **Optuna** (40 trials, 5-fold time-series CV, val_quantile=0.8). The tuned parameters — `iterations=307, depth=10, learning_rate=0.147, l2_leaf_reg=19.6, bagging_temperature=1.17` — are not magic, they're just what the search found. Final metrics on the held-out fraudTest:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| **PR-AUC (val)** | 0.9112 |
| **PR-AUC (test)** | 0.8384 |
| ROC-AUC (test) | 0.9938 |
| F1 @ T2 threshold | 0.7352 |

The gap between val and test (0.91 → 0.84) is a real drift signal. I'll come back to that.

## Three-tier decision logic: the business layer

A single `fraud_probability = 0.62` is useless to a fraud analyst. They want a decision. So I built a three-tier threshold layer on top of the model output:

| Tier | Threshold | Action | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T1 auto_block** | ≥ 0.5613 | Block transaction immediately | High-confidence fraud. PR=0.95, R=0.52 → mostly catches the big-ticket fraud. |
| **T2 review_queue** | ≥ 0.1198 | Send to human review queue | Medium confidence. Catches most of the rest. |
| **T3 soft_signal** | ≥ 0.0040 | Tag for downstream risk scoring | Anything the model thinks is non-trivial. |
| **T0 approve** | < 0.0040 | Approve | 99.6% of legit transactions. |

The thresholds aren't symmetric — T1 is a "block" decision that needs high precision, T2 is "review" that can tolerate more recall, T3 is just a tag. I picked them by sweeping the precision-recall curve and asking "where does the marginal cost of a false positive exceed the marginal cost of a false negative?" That's a business call, not a math call.

I wrote this as a `PredictionPipeline` class that takes the raw Sparkov 14 features, runs feature engineering, scores, and returns `{proba, tier, action, threshold_used, transaction_id}`. The same class is used by the FastAPI server, the Kafka consumer, and the demo (Run 100 / Run 1000) — so business logic is in exactly one place.

## The MLOps stack

This is where most "data science projects" stop and "production ML systems" begin. The pieces I built:

### 1. DVC — data + pipeline versioning

I used [DVC](https://dvc.org/) to version both the data and the pipeline. `dvc.yaml` declares 8 stages with explicit `deps` and `outs`:

```yaml
stages:
  ingest_postgres:
    cmd: python -m src.components.data_ingestion_postgres
    deps: [data/raw/fraudTrain.csv, data/raw/fraudTest.csv, ...]
  feast_ingest:
    cmd: python -m src.components.data_ingestion_feast
    outs: [data/sparkov_cc_features.parquet, ...]
  train:
    cmd: python -m pipelines.training_pipeline
    params: [model.iterations, model.depth, model.learning_rate, ...]
    metrics: [metrics/train_metrics.json]
  promote:
    cmd: python -m src.components.mlflow_promote --floor 0.78
```

Why DVC over Git LFS? Because it understands the pipeline. If `data/raw/fraudTest.csv` hasn't changed, `dvc repro train` skips the train step. If I bump `params.yaml:model.iterations`, only train re-runs, not the data ingestion. And `dvc metrics show` gives you a git-history of model performance.

The data lives in **DAGsHub S3** (DAGsHub gives you a free MLflow + DVC remote for open-source projects). Tracking 500 MB of CSVs + 2.4 MB of model artifacts works fine.

> **Update:** DVC is now data-versioning only. The pipeline was replaced by **Apache Airflow** (see Phase 7 below) so the scheduler can be cron-based, drift-triggered, and have a real UI with retries + observability.

### 2. MLflow + DAGsHub — experiment tracking + model registry

Every training run logs to MLflow: 9 metrics (val/test PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, F1, tier1/2 P/R), the 3-tier thresholds, the model artifact (`catboost.cbm`), the feature engineering pickle, and the run parameters. Model versions are promoted to "Production" with a single CLI call:

```bash
python -m src.components.mlflow_promote --floor 0.78
```

This script does three things:
1. Reads `metrics/train_metrics.json` for the current run's PR-AUC
2. Compares to the current Production model's PR-AUC
3. Promotes Staging → Production **only if** the new model is better

DAGsHub's free MLflow tier doesn't support the new `set_registered_model_alias` API. I found that out the hard way after registering the model and having the alias silently fail to persist. The fix: use the legacy `transition_model_version_stage(name, version, 'Production')` API. It works everywhere. The rollback workflow does the same thing in reverse.

### 3. Feast — online/offline feature consistency

This is the architecture decision I'm most proud of. I built **32 features** during training. The problem: 13 of them are velocity features (`txn_last_1h`, `amt_sum_last_24h`, `amt_per_cc_num_mean`...) that require the **history of all past transactions on the card and merchant**. You can't compute them from a single row at inference time.

I split the features into two groups:

| Type | Examples | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **Static** (computable from one row) | `hour`, `day_of_week`, `distance_from_home`, `age`, `amt_log` | Embedded in the model, computed in the API |
| **Velocity** (requires history) | `txn_last_1h`, `amt_sum_last_24h`, `cc_num_FE`, `merchant_te` | Feast → Redis |

Feast gives you a **single source of truth**: you define a feature view once, and Feast generates both an offline table (for training, via DuckDB) and an online store (for inference, via Redis). When you do `training_pipeline.featurize(df)`, Feast applies point-in-time correct joins so you don't leak. When the API does `feature_store.get_online_features_for_batch(df)`, you get the latest Redis values.

The two implementations use the **same Python code path** to compute the features. If you change the rolling window from 24h to 6h, training and inference both update. That was the entire point.

### 4. FastAPI — the inference service

8 endpoints. 2 of them are interesting:

```python
@app.post("/predict")
async def predict(transaction: TransactionRequest, request: Request):
    # 1. Pandera schema validation — coerce bad types, log warnings, never block
    df = validate_transactions(pd.DataFrame([transaction.model_dump()]))
    # 2. Run the pipeline (Feast + CatBoost + 3-tier)
    out = pipeline.predict(df)
    # 3. Audit log → Postgres
    insert_decision(trans_num=..., proba=..., tier=..., action=...)
    # 4. Broadcast to WebSocket subscribers (the dashboard)
    await manager.broadcast(response.model_dump())
    return response
```

Three production-grade features that you don't get for free:
- **Pandera schema validation** at the boundary. A negative `amt` gets logged and coerced; a malformed date gets a warning. The model never sees garbage.
- **Postgres audit log** — one row per `/predict`, with the request IP, user agent, and latency. The regulator-friendly version of the decision log.
- **WebSocket broadcast** to the React dashboard. Live fraud-rate chart updates in <100ms.

### 5. Kafka — the streaming pipeline

For the "real-time" angle, I built a Kafka producer/consumer pair. The producer reads 100 sample transactions from fraudTest and publishes to `fraud.events`. The consumer scores them, writes to Postgres, and broadcasts to the dashboard. KRaft mode (no Zookeeper), single broker, Apache Kafka 3.7. End-to-end latency: 5 ms per transaction.

The Kafka library situation on Python 3.12+ is rough. `kafka-python==2.0.2` is unmaintained and broken. Use `kafka-python-ng==2.2.3` instead — same API, Python 3.12 wheels.

### 6. The unified React dashboard

The most user-facing piece. 3 tabs, single React app at `http://localhost:3000`:

| Tab | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **📊 Live** | Real-time fraud-rate chart, live feed, metrics bar |
| **🧪 Demo** | Run 100 or 1000 tests, see confusion matrix + tier summary |
| **🌊 Drift** | Embedded Evidently report (train vs test) |

The **▶ Run 100** and **▶ Run 1000** buttons in the header trigger `/demo/run-100-tests` with different sample sizes and auto-switch to the Demo tab when finished. The 1000-test run returns summary-only (confusion matrix, F1, tier counts) — individual result rows are dropped to keep the payload small. Monitoring, auditing, and model-registry concerns moved to dedicated tools: **Grafana + Prometheus** for observability, **Postgres audit log** for decision history, and **MLflow on DAGsHub** for the model registry.

### 7. CI/CD — GitHub Actions

Two workflows. CI runs on every push + PR: ruff lint, compile check, parity test, API test, frontend build. CD runs on every push to `main`: `dvc pull` dataset, lint, test, and (optionally) trigger an Airflow retraining DAG stub. Actual model training and promotion run on a schedule via Airflow, not on every push — avoiding wasted compute and DAGsHub API rate limits.

A third workflow, `rollback.yml`, is `workflow_dispatch`-triggered: it moves the previous Production model back, archives the current one, and writes a `rollback_log.json` for the audit trail.

## The challenges I hit (and what they taught me)

A "look how it works" blog post is worthless. The interesting part is the things that didn't work.

### Challenge 1: The fraud rate is 0.5%

The biggest modeling constraint. With 1,000 positives out of 200,000, accuracy is meaningless (predict "legit" for everything and you get 99.5% accuracy). I tracked PR-AUC, F1, and per-tier precision/recall instead of accuracy or ROC-AUC. Class weighting helped the tree models slightly; the bigger win was choosing the right metric to optimize.

**The lesson:** Pick the metric that reflects the business cost before you train your first model. Re-tuning hyperparameters with the wrong metric is a waste of a weekend.

### Challenge 2: The 32 features can't all live in the model

13 of my features are velocity features — they require history. My first attempt was to compute them at inference time by reading from Redis. That worked, but the training-time and inference-time implementations were **different code paths**, which is the #1 way to get leakage in production.

**The lesson:** Use a feature store. Feast isn't free to learn, but the cost of "training and inference don't agree" is much higher.

### Challenge 3: MLflow Model Registry on DAGsHub is weird

DAGsHub gives you free MLflow tracking + S3 storage, which is incredible for open-source projects. But their MLflow server doesn't support `set_registered_model_alias` (the new v2.9+ API) — silently fails to persist. I had to use the legacy `transition_model_version_stage` API for both promotion and rollback. Annoying, but it works.

**The lesson:** When using a hosted MLflow, test the model registry *before* you build a CI/CD pipeline on top of it. The CI doesn't fail loudly when the alias doesn't persist — it just doesn't take effect.

### Challenge 4: DAGsHub MLflow rate-limits back-to-back runs

The CD workflow trains and then immediately tries to promote. DAGsHub's free tier rate-limits MLflow API calls; back-to-back runs fail with 403. I added `continue-on-error: true` to the train step and a 60-second sleep before the promote step. Crude, but works.

**The lesson:** Free hosted ML services always have hidden rate limits. For a real production system, self-host MLflow or pay for the managed tier.

### Challenge 5: Pandera 0.31.x API broke

I started with the legacy `pa.dataframe_parser().dtype` API, which is now removed. Switched to `pa.DataFrameModel` with `pa.Field(ge=, le=, isin=)` and `Config: strict=False, coerce=True`. The errors come back as JSON via `lazy=True` and the pipeline can recover from warnings. The schema catches negative `amt` and 3-letter `state` codes — not glamorous, but it's what stops bad data from corrupting the model.

**The lesson:** Schema validation at the API boundary is the cheapest insurance you can buy. 60 lines of Pandera, 5 fewer pages of postmortem reports.

### Challenge 6: The 500 MB CSVs don't fit in git

`data/raw/fraudTest.csv` is 150 MB. `fraudTrain.csv` is 350 MB. Both gitignored, both required for training. I tracked them in DVC (`dvc add`, `dvc push` → DAGsHub S3), then the CD workflow does `dvc pull` with the DAGsHub token as the AWS credentials. Took 3 iterations of CI failures to get the S3 auth right — DAGsHub uses your token as both `AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID` and `AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY`, and DVC's S3 backend picks up standard boto3 env vars automatically.

**The lesson:** If you have a 500 MB dataset, you need a real data store. Don't try to make git do it. DVC + S3 is the cheap-and-good answer; lakeFS, Iceberg, or Delta Lake are the expensive-and-good answers.

### Challenge 7: CI YAML is fragile

I tried to inline a multi-line Python script in a YAML string with `python -c "..."`. YAML interpreted the opening `"` as the start of a quoted scalar and choked on the next line. Extracted the smoke test to `scripts/s3_smoke_test.py` and called it from the workflow. Lesson: keep the YAML dumb, put the logic in code.

### Challenge 8: Ruff lint blocking every commit

The repo accumulated 67 ruff errors over time (unused imports, missing `import joblib`, etc.) and the CI lint step was failing on every push. I spent 10 minutes running `ruff check --fix` (handled 58 of them) and fixed the remaining 9 by hand. The real bug was `src/components/schema_validation.py` had `joblib.load(...)` without `import joblib` — would have crashed at runtime the first time someone ran the smoke test.

**The lesson:** CI lint failures are not just style problems. Sometimes the linter catches real bugs. Take the time.

### Challenge 9: The Dependabot PRs from before the lint fix

After the lint fix, the 9 Dependabot PRs (auto-bumping fastapi, pydantic, uvicorn, etc.) were still based on the old main with the lint errors. Re-running alone doesn't help — they need to rebase onto the new main. Dependabot's `rebase-strategy: auto` should handle this, but it sometimes doesn't for very stale PRs. The cleanest workaround: close them and let Dependabot recreate them on its next weekly scan.

**The lesson:** Bots that operate on old base branches create work for you. Set up auto-rebase, or accept that you'll close-and-reopen every few months.

## The results

Final metrics on the held-out fraudTest:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| **PR-AUC (val)** | **0.9112** |
| **PR-AUC (test)** | **0.8384** |
| ROC-AUC (test) | 0.9938 |
| T1 precision / recall | 0.9502 / 0.5161 |
| T2 precision / recall | 0.6525 / 0.8420 |
| T3 precision / recall | 0.1929 / 0.9510 |
| 100-test macro F1 | 0.9400 |
| API P95 latency | 50 ms |

Production registry:
- **v3 = Production** (test_pr_auc=0.8384, promoted via CI)
- v1 = Archived (was the baseline; auto_block precision 0.78)
- v2 = Staging (the same tuned model as v3 — different MLflow run)

The test PR-AUC (0.84) is meaningfully lower than val (0.91). That's a real drift signal. The drift report (Evidently, train vs test) confirms it: 14 of 32 features show statistical drift (KS test p < 0.05). FraudTest is from 6 months after fraudTrain, and the merchant mix, transaction patterns, and amounts have shifted. The next iteration of this project should add a periodic retraining step — but that's out of scope for a demo.

### Phase 7 (added later): Apache Airflow for orchestration

The "next iteration" above is now in the repo. Two DAGs in `airflow/dags/`:

- **`fraud_drift_check`** (daily 06:00 UTC) — runs `src.components.drift_report`, parses the JSON for the number of features that drifted (KS p<0.05), and triggers…
- **`fraud_retraining`** (weekly Mon 00:00 UTC + drift-triggered) — `dvc pull` → `pipelines.training_pipeline` → metric gate (test_pr_auc ≥ 0.78) → `mlflow_promote` → `feast apply` + `feast materialize`.

DAGs are containerised in `airflow/Dockerfile` (extends `apache/airflow:2.10.4-python3.12`); bring the stack up with `docker compose -f airflow/docker-compose.yml up -d`, then open `http://localhost:8080` (admin / admin). DVC is now data-versioning only — the `dvc.yaml` file is empty and `dvc repro` is no longer used.

## What I'd do differently next time

A few things that, in retrospect, I should have done:

1. **Self-host MLflow.** DAGsHub's free tier is great, but the rate limits and missing model-alias API are operational headaches. A 4-line Docker Compose for MLflow + Postgres is a one-time setup that removes both.
2. **Add a retraining step to CI.** The CD workflow retrains on every push, which is too often. A weekly scheduled workflow that pulls 90 days of data and retrains is more realistic.
3. **Add a feature-drift detector in CI.** Right now I have data drift (Evidently) and metric drift (PR-AUC < 0.78), but not feature-level drift at inference. That's the gap between "the model is bad" and "this specific prediction was bad".
4. **Skip Kafka for the demo.** It's impressive, but the value it adds over WebSocket + a SQL audit log is small for a single-machine demo. Keep Kafka for the actual production version that runs on 3+ nodes.
5. **Add a baseline "dummy" model in the registry** that always returns 0.5. Then your PR-AUC delta is meaningful — you're comparing to random, not to "the model I trained last week but lost the run ID for".

## Why this matters to me

I came into this project thinking "fraud detection" was a clever modeling problem. By the end, I realized it's an **integration problem**. The model is 5% of the work. The other 95% is the data versioning, the feature store, the schema validation, the model registry, the audit log, the drift detection, the rollback workflow, the dashboard that lets a non-ML person understand what the model is doing.

That's the real takeaway. If you're a recruiter reading this: I can build the modeling part, but what I'm most proud of is the integration part — making sure the model is **trustable, observable, and reversible** when it goes wrong. That distinction matters for senior IC and staff-level roles, and it's what separates a Kaggle notebook from a system that ships.
